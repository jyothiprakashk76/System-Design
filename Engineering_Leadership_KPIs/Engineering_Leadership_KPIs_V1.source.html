What to measure, what it is paired with, and what never to measure at all

1  ·  WHAT YOU ARE ACTUALLY ACCOUNTABLE FOR — AND THE TRAP AT THIS LEVEL start here — engineering metrics alone do not earn credibility with the business, and business metrics alone are not actionable by engineers
The four things a Director owns
DeliveryDoes work reach production predictably, in small safe increments?
ReliabilityDo the journeys that make money keep working?
CostDoes unit economics improve as you scale?
PeopleCan the organisation still do this in two years?
The trap, and it catches most people at this level. Report only DORA metrics and your CEO hears nothing they care about — deployment frequency is not a business outcome. Report only revenue and conversion and your engineers cannot act on it. Your job is the translation layer: connecting a latency percentile to a conversion rate, and an error budget to a decision about whether to ship.
So the structure that works is layered. A business outcome sits on top of a journey-level service objective, which sits on an engineering KPI, which sits on a team-level signal. Each layer explains the one above and is actionable by the layer below — panel 4 sets this out.
The one-line test for any proposed KPI
“If this number moves, what decision changes?” If nobody can answer, it is a dashboard ornament. Delete it.
Choosing them
  • Pick five to eight. A director tracking forty KPIs is tracking none. Choose the ones tied to this year's constraint
  • Retire them when the constraint moves. KPIs are instruments, not monuments. A metric nobody has acted on in two quarters is noise
  • Every KPI gets a counter-metric — see panel 14. A metric optimised alone always produces a pathology
  • Percentiles, not averages. The average request is fine; the p99 is the one losing you customers
  • Trend over target in the first year. Direction and consistency beat an invented threshold
  • Instrument the journey, not the service. Every service at 99.9% can still mean a broken checkout
Leading versus lagging, and why you need both. Lagging metrics — incident count, attrition, revenue impact — tell you what already happened and are what the business asks about. Leading metrics — error budget burn, PR review latency, on-call load, dependency age — tell you what is about to happen, and are the ones you can still act on. A dashboard of only lagging indicators is a rear-view mirror.
How this sheet is organised
Panels 2–7Universal. These apply whatever you build — delivery, reliability, cost, security, tech health and people
Panels 8–13Domain-specific. Six product types, each with the small number of KPIs that genuinely matter and one marked ★ as the headline
Panels 14–16Counter-metrics, what to report upward, the anti-patterns, and an operating cadence
A note on benchmarks. This sheet deliberately gives no target numbers — no “deploy N times a day”, no “p95 under N milliseconds”. Those depend entirely on your domain, scale, regulatory context and architecture, and a borrowed number is worse than none because it manufactures false confidence. Measure your own baseline first, then set a target against it.
The honest framing for your first ninety days: most organisations cannot measure half of this. Start by finding out which ones you genuinely cannot produce — that list is itself the finding, and it is usually a more useful first report than any of the numbers.
2  ·  DELIVERY — THROUGHPUT AND SAFETY, WHICH MUST BE READ TOGETHER UNIVERSAL the four research-backed measures, plus the flow metrics that explain them
KPIWhat it actually tells you
Deployment frequencyA proxy for batch size and confidence. Falling frequency almost always means fear, not less work — investigate it as a signal about the team, not the pipeline
Lead time for change
commit → production		Report p50 and p90 together. The p50 flatters you; the p90 is where the painful path lives, and it is the one engineers experience
Change failure rateShare of deploys causing degradation or requiring remediation. The safety half of the pair — deployment frequency without it is meaningless
Failed-deployment recovery timeHow quickly you undo a bad change. Often a better investment than preventing the change from failing
Read the first and third together, always. High deployment frequency with a high change failure rate is recklessness. Low frequency with a low failure rate is usually not safety — it is large batches that simply have not failed yet. The healthy pattern is both improving at once, which is the finding that made these four measures interesting in the first place.
Flow metrics — where the lead time actually goes
PR review latency
open → first review		Usually the single largest hidden component of lead time, and the cheapest to fix. Measure it before buying any tooling
PR size distributionLarge PRs cause slow reviews, which cause larger PRs. Breaking that loop moves several other metrics at once
Work in progress per teamHigh WIP is context-switching made visible. Reducing it raises throughput without adding people
Flaky test rateErodes trust in the pipeline. Once engineers re-run failures by reflex, your safety net is gone
Build and test duration p90Directly sets how small a batch can reasonably be
% work unplannedInterrupt load. Above roughly a third, planning is fiction and your estimates will keep being wrong
The diagnostic sequence that works. Lead time is high → break it into coding, review wait, build, and release wait → in most organisations review wait dominates → fix that before anything else. It is a working-agreement change, not a budget request.
3  ·  RELIABILITY journeys, not servers
Raw uptime is close to meaningless. Every service can report 99.9% while checkout is broken. Define service objectives on user journeys — complete a purchase, send a message, join a call — and measure those. This single change makes reliability legible to the business.
KPINotes
SLO attainment per critical journeyThe headline. One number per journey that the business recognises
Error budget burn rateThe most useful mechanism at your level. It converts reliability from an argument into a rule: budget spent means stabilise, budget healthy means ship faster
Incidents by severity, trendRaw count is noise. The severity mix is the signal
MTTD — time to detectUsually the worst of the three and the cheapest to improve. Most “slow recovery” is slow detection
MTTA — time to acknowledgeTests your paging path, not your engineers
MTTR — time to restoreRestore, not root-cause. Those are different activities
% incidents self-detectedVersus reported by a customer. A blunt, honest measure of observability quality
Repeat-incident rateSame cause recurring means postmortem actions are not landing
Postmortem action closureClosure rate, not write-up rate. Unclosed actions are a promise nobody kept
The two-metric version, if you can only have two: SLO attainment on the revenue-critical journey, and percentage of incidents you detected before a customer told you. Between them they cover whether the product works and whether you would know if it did not.
Track toil separately — the share of time spent on manual, repetitive, automatable work. Sustained above roughly thirty percent and your engineering team has become a support desk, which shows up as attrition long before it shows up in delivery metrics.
4  ·  THE MEASUREMENT MODEL — FOUR LAYERS, AND WHAT EACH AUDIENCE SEES how a business outcome connects to something an engineer can act on, and which numbers travel upward
5  ·  COST AND UNIT ECONOMICS — THE LANGUAGE THAT TRAVELS HIGH LEVERAGE the fastest way to be taken seriously outside engineering
KPIWhy it earns attention
Unit cost
per order, viewing hour, message, participant-minute		The single most useful number you can produce. It converts infrastructure into a figure finance already reasons about, and it makes an architecture improvement legible as a margin improvement
Infra cost as % of revenue, trendShould decline as you scale. If it is flat, you have an architecture problem, not a procurement problem — and this is the chart that proves it either way
Cost per team and per serviceAttribution must exist before optimisation is possible. Showing teams their own number reduces spend before any technical change
Cost of a percentage point of reliabilityMakes the “why not five nines” conversation quantitative rather than cultural
Idle and over-provisioned capacityUsually the largest single line, and the least contentious to reclaim
The framing that works with engineers: cost is a design constraint, not a procurement problem. A partitioning choice, a cache hit rate, a retry policy and an instance type are all cost decisions made by engineers — and they move the number far more than renegotiating the contract does.
Tech health — the debt that is not a metaphor
% services on supported versionsRuntimes, frameworks, databases, base images. Your deferred-maintenance liability in one number, and the one that eventually blocks a security fix
Dependency age — median and worstA leading indicator. The worst case is usually the thing that will make an upgrade urgent and expensive later
Migration completion %For each in-flight migration, with a date. Half-finished migrations are the most expensive state — you pay for both systems and get the benefits of neither
Services without an ownerShould be zero. Anything above zero is an incident waiting for a weekend
Toil %Manual repetitive work. Predicts attrition earlier than any engagement survey
The number to watch for a stalled migration. Track completion percentage with a date, and escalate when it stops moving. A migration that is eighty percent done for three quarters is a strategic failure that will not surface in any other metric — delivery and reliability can both look healthy while it quietly doubles your operating surface.
6  ·  SECURITY & COMPLIANCE measurable, not aspirational
KPINotes
Time to patch critical vulnerabilitiesAgainst a stated SLA, and report adherence rather than the raw count. This is the one that goes upward
Open critical and high findings, ageingThe ageing distribution matters more than the total — a small number of very old findings is worse than many new ones
% services with scanning and SBOMCoverage of the programme itself. Uncovered services are where the surprise lives
Secret exposure incidentsShould be zero. Also measure time to detect and rotate — exposure happens, undetected exposure is the failure
% of production images signed and verifiedSupply-chain posture, expressed as a percentage rather than a policy document
Access review completionStanding privileged access is the finding auditors reach for first
Audit or certification findings, and repeat findingsA repeat finding is far more damaging than a new one — it says the previous remediation was cosmetic
Recovery objectives provenNot stated — proven by a tested restore, with the measured time. An untested backup is a hypothesis
Report adherence, not activity. “Ninety-four percent of critical vulnerabilities patched within SLA, worst outstanding at eleven days” is a sentence an executive can act on. “We closed six hundred findings” is not — it says nothing about what remains.
The one that catches organisations out is proven recovery. Everyone has backups; far fewer have restored one this year and timed it. Make the measured restore time a KPI, because it is the number your recovery objective actually depends on — and the one that turns out to be wrong during an incident.
7  ·  PEOPLE & ORGANISATION the leading indicators
KPINotes
Regretted attritionSeparate it from total attrition, which is nearly meaningless — losing a poor fit and losing your best architect are not the same event. Report regretted only
On-call load distributionPages per engineer per week, and out-of-hours share. Concentration here predicts your next resignation more reliably than any survey
Pages per on-call shift, trendRising means alerting quality is degrading. Falling to near zero may mean alerts were silenced rather than fixed
Time to first meaningful contributionMeasures your onboarding and documentation, not the hire
Internal mobility ratePeople moving between teams is a health signal. Zero mobility means teams are hoarding
Bus factor on critical systemsCount systems with a single knowledgeable owner. This is a risk register, not a vanity metric
Engineering time on planned workThe inverse of interrupt load, and the honest explanation for a missed roadmap
Hiring pipeline healthTime to hire, offer acceptance rate, funnel drop-off by stage
On-call load is the most under-used metric on this sheet. It is a leading indicator of attrition, an honest measure of system health, and a direct consequence of engineering decisions — unlike an engagement score, which tells you sentiment has already deteriorated. It is also one of the few numbers where the fix and the measure sit in the same team's hands.
Never measure individuals with any metric on this sheet. Commits, lines, story points, review counts — all of them reward volume over judgement and destroy the collaboration that produces good systems. Team and system level only.
8  ·  E-COMMERCE conversion is the frame
KPIWhy
★ Checkout success rateThe headline. Every fraction of a percent is direct revenue, and it is the number the business already understands
Payment success rate
by gateway and method		Split it — isolates a failing provider from a failing checkout. Aggregate success rate hides a single broken method
Duplicate order rateIdempotency failures. A correctness bug that costs refunds and trust, and one nobody notices until a customer complains
Oversell rateInventory consistency in one number — and a direct customer-experience failure
Product and search page latency p95Tie it explicitly to conversion. The latency-to-revenue link is the most reliable argument you have for performance work
Search zero-result rateUsually the largest untapped conversion lever, and cheap to measure
Cart abandonment from technical causesSeparated from ordinary abandonment. Only the technical share is yours
Order-to-fulfilment successWhere asynchronous failures hide — the order succeeded and then quietly did not progress
Peak headroomCapacity divided by forecast peak, before a sale event. The metric that prevents the annual incident
Refund and chargeback SLAOperational load and a regulatory exposure
The conversation this enables: “Checkout success is 97.2%. Recovering one point is worth X in revenue, and the top cause is one payment provider's timeout behaviour.” That is an engineering finding stated as a business case — and it will be funded.
9  ·  STREAMING quality of experience
KPIWhy
★ Rebuffering ratioThe dominant quality metric. Time spent buffering as a share of viewing time. It correlates with abandonment more strongly than any other technical measure
Time to first frame
startup time, p50 and p95		Drives abandonment before playback even begins. The p95 is where the lost viewers are
Video start failure rateDistinct from rebuffering — the stream never began at all. A different failure with a different cause
Exit before video startYour startup experience expressed as lost viewers rather than milliseconds
Average delivered bitrateQuality actually served, versus quality encoded. The gap is your delivery problem
CDN offload ratioShare served from edge rather than origin. Drives both cost and latency
Cost per GB delivered and per viewing hourYour largest bill, and the two ways to talk about it
DRM licence failure rateSmall number, disproportionate support load, and highly visible to customers
Encoding throughput and cost per content-hourPipeline efficiency, and the constraint on catalogue growth
Peak concurrent streams vs capacityEspecially for live events, where the peak is known in advance and unforgiving
Segment quality metrics by device, geography and network. An acceptable aggregate rebuffering ratio routinely conceals one platform or one region having an unusable experience — and that segment is where your churn is concentrated.
10  ·  SOCIAL PLATFORM content in, content out
KPIWhy
★ Post publish success rateNever lose a user's content. A failed publish is the single most damaging experience on a social product
Media upload success rate and processing timeThe highest-friction path in most social apps, and disproportionately mobile-network dependent
Notification delivery rate and latencySplit iOS and Android — platform behaviour differs enough that an aggregate number misleads
Graph query latency p99Follower and friend traversals are the hardest thing you scale. The p99 is the account with many connections
Moderation time to action, by severityIncreasingly a regulatory metric, not merely an operational one. Track the severe categories separately
Moderation false-positive rateThe counter-metric. Without it you optimise speed into over-blocking, and creators leave
Abuse detection precision and recallReport both. Either alone is trivially gamed in opposite directions
Appeal volume and overturn rateA high overturn rate means your enforcement is wrong, not that users are complaining
Availability weighted by trafficAn outage at peak is not equivalent to one at the quietest hour; weight accordingly
Moderation is where engineering KPIs meet regulatory exposure. Time to action on severe categories is increasingly something you must be able to evidence to a regulator. Build the measurement before you are asked for it — retrofitting it under a deadline is materially harder.
11  ·  CHAT & MESSAGING correctness before latency
KPIWhy
★ Message delivery success rateMust approach one hundred percent. Measure it explicitly end to end — do not infer it from service availability
Message loss rateShould be zero. Discovering a non-zero number you did not know about is the worst finding available in this domain
Delivery latency p50 / p95 / p99The p99 is the conversation that felt broken
Ordering violation rateOut-of-order messages are a correctness bug users notice immediately and report as the product being broken
Duplicate delivery rateThe other half of exactly-once, and equally visible
Connection drop rate and reconnect successMobile networks make this your real reliability story — drops are inevitable, failed reconnects are not
Offline sync time and successThe experience after a tunnel or a flight, and a common source of perceived loss
Push notification delivery ratePer platform. Often the weakest link in perceived delivery
Media attachment success rateSeparate from text; different failure profile entirely
Group fan-out latency by group sizeLarge groups are where the architecture is tested
This is the one domain where correctness ranks above latency. Users forgive a message arriving a second late; they do not forgive one arriving twice, out of order, or not at all. Order your KPIs accordingly — and make loss rate a number someone looks at weekly, not one you assume is zero.
12  ·  VIDEO CALLING connect first, then quality
KPIWhy
★ Call join success rateFailing to connect is far worse than degraded quality. A poor call is an annoyance; a call that will not start is a product failure
Call setup timeTime from join to media flowing. Directly shapes the perception of the product
Call drop rateSeparated from user-ended calls — which requires deliberate instrumentation
Mean opinion scoreThe standard audio-quality measure and your headline experience number
Packet loss, jitter, round-trip timeDistributions, never averages. The average conceals exactly the sessions that were bad
Video freeze rate and durationWhat users actually describe as a bad call
Audio-video sync driftSmall drift is more irritating to users than lower resolution
Relay usage shareYour main cost driver. Relayed calls cost far more than direct peer connections — track the share and what pushes it up
Cost per participant-minuteThe unit economics of the product
Join success by network typeCorporate networks and restrictive firewalls are where joins fail
Rank these deliberately: join success, then drop rate, then quality. Teams routinely optimise resolution and bitrate while a measurable share of calls never connect at all — and the second group churns, while the first merely complains.
13  ·  NEWS FEED AND TIMELINE — THE FAN-OUT PROBLEM, MEASURED Twitter-shaped products · STAFF the tail is the architecture, so measure it separately
KPIWhy
★ Timeline generation latency p99The defining technical constraint of the product. Everything in the architecture exists to hold this number down
Feed freshness
post → visible to followers		What your fan-out strategy actually optimises, and the number users perceive as the product being live
Fan-out completion for high-follower accountsTrack the tail separately from the median. The celebrity case is a different system from the ordinary one, and averaging them hides both
Timeline cache hit rateDrives latency and cost simultaneously — the highest-leverage single number after p99 latency
Ranking inference latencyUsually the dominant contributor to the p99 once caching is working
Write amplification ratioFan-out writes per post. Capacity and cost expressed as one number
Hot key and hot partition incidentsYour recurring structural failure. Count them; the trend tells you whether mitigation is working
Search and trend indexing lagHow stale discovery is relative to posting
Why the tail deserves its own metric here more than in any other domain. A feed product has two populations that behave nothing alike — the ordinary account with a modest following, and the small number of accounts whose every post triggers enormous fan-out. A single median latency figure describes neither, and the second population is where both your incidents and your most visible users are. Report them as separate series.
Engagement metrics you should be careful with
Time in feed, session lengthProduct metrics, not engineering ones. You enable them; you do not own them — and claiming them invites a conversation you cannot win
Engagement rateDriven by ranking and content, not by reliability. Useful as context for capacity planning, misleading as an engineering KPI
What you can legitimately own: that the feed loads quickly, is fresh, is available, ranks within its latency budget, and costs a predictable amount per user per day. Engagement is the product organisation's outcome, and being precise about that boundary is part of the job — it protects you when engagement dips for reasons that have nothing to do with engineering.
Capacity planning here is event-driven. Feed products have sharp, sometimes predictable peaks — a major news event, a sporting final, a product launch. Peak headroom against a forecast belongs on your dashboard alongside the latency numbers, for the same reason it does in e-commerce.
14  ·  COUNTER-METRICS every KPI needs a guard
Any metric optimised alone produces a pathology. This is not cynicism about people — it is what happens when you point an organisation at a single number. The fix is structural: publish each KPI with the metric that would degrade if it were gamed, and review the pair together.
Primary KPIMust be paired with
Deployment frequencyChange failure rate — otherwise you are rewarding recklessness
Lead timeEscaped defect rate — speed achieved by skipping review is not speed
Incident count trending downMTTD — you may simply be detecting fewer, not causing fewer
MTTR trending downRepeat-incident rate — fast restoration of the same fault is not improvement
LatencyCost per request — latency is always purchasable with money
Cost reductionSLO attainment — savings taken out of reliability arrive as an outage later
Moderation time to actionFalse-positive rate — speed alone produces over-blocking
Test coverageEscaped defect rate — coverage is trivially inflated with assertion-free tests
Throughput or velocityRework and defect rate — output is not outcome
UtilisationLead time — high utilisation lengthens queues; a fully loaded system is a slow one
Alert volume down% incidents self-detected — alerts may have been silenced rather than fixed
Make the pairing visible on the dashboard itself, side by side rather than on separate pages. The discipline is not in choosing good metrics — it is in never showing one without its guard, so the trade-off is present in every conversation rather than discovered in a retrospective.
15  ·  WHAT NEVER TO MEASURE — AND THE FAILURE MODES OF A METRICS PROGRAMME the things that actively cause harm, and the ways a good programme quietly stops working
Never measure these
MetricWhy it causes harm
Lines of codeRewards verbosity and punishes deletion — the most valuable change a senior engineer makes
Commit or PR countsTrivially inflated by splitting work. Measures activity, never outcome
Story points or velocity as performanceInflates within one sprint of being watched. It is a planning aid for a team, and worthless the moment it leaves the team
Any individual developer metricDestroys collaboration faster than anything else here. Helping a colleague, reviewing carefully and mentoring all reduce your personal numbers
Test coverage as a targetGoodhart's law in its purest form — you get tests that assert nothing
Raw uptime without journey SLOs99.9% while checkout is broken is a number that misleads everyone who reads it
Hours worked or presenceMeasures compliance, not contribution, and selects for the wrong people
How a good programme rots
FailureWhat it looks like
Metric creepForty numbers on a dashboard, none reviewed. Add a retirement rule when you add the metric
Team signals used for appraisalThe fastest way to destroy the programme. Engineers optimise the measure, and you lose both the number and the trust that produced it
Vanity reporting upwardOnly improving metrics reach the board. The first genuine problem then arrives as a surprise, and your credibility goes with it
Averages everywhereEvery percentile-shaped problem becomes invisible
Borrowed benchmarksAnother organisation's numbers encode their domain and architecture. Chasing them produces motion without direction
Measuring what is easyThe available number displaces the important one. Build-duration dashboards while nobody measures checkout success
No counter-metricsEvery headline number drifts toward its pathology within two quarters
Signals your programme is working
  • A metric changed a decision this quarter — the only test that matters
  • Teams look at their own numbers without being asked, and argue about them
  • You have retired a KPI, not only added ones
  • A number went the wrong way and you reported it upward anyway — and the conversation was productive
  • Product plans against your error budget rather than negotiating reliability after the fact
  • An engineer cites a KPI in a design review unprompted
The single question to ask of any dashboard: “when did this last change what we did?” If the honest answer is never, the dashboard is providing the appearance of oversight rather than oversight — which is worse than having none, because it stops anyone looking further.
And the one cultural rule that protects everything above: metrics are used to improve systems, never to rank people. State it explicitly, repeatedly, and act consistently with it — the first time a number appears in a performance conversation, every number you collect afterwards becomes less true.

The one that summarises the sheet: name the business outcome you are accountable to, the journey SLO that supports it, the engineering KPI that moves that SLO, and the counter-metric that stops the KPI being gamed. If you can state that chain for your top priority, the rest of this sheet is detail. If you cannot, no number on it will help — because measurement without that chain is reporting, not management.
