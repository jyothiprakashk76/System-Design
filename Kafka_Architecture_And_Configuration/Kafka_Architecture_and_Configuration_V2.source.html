1  ·  WHAT KAFKA IS, AND WHY IT EXISTS start here — the problem it was built for, before the configuration
The definition
Kafka is a distributed, append-only, replicated commit log. Producers append records to the end of a log; consumers read forward from a position they choose and remember. Nothing is removed when it is read.
The one distinction that matters: a queue delivers a message, gets an acknowledgement, and deletes it. A log appends records and keeps them for a retention period, while each consumer tracks its own offset. Almost every design mistake with Kafka comes from treating it as a queue.
What follows from being a log
  • Many independent consumers read the same records without competing — each has its own offset.
  • Replay is ordinary. Reset an offset and reprocess history; you are not recovering a deleted message.
  • Order is per partition, never across a topic. This is the constraint everything else is designed around.
  • Consumers can be slow or offline without blocking producers — the log is the buffer.
Why it is fast
  • Sequential writes to an append-only file, not random updates.
  • Batching and compression across many records rather than per message.
  • Consumers pull, so a slow consumer never overwhelms itself.
  • Partitions parallelise — throughput scales by adding partitions and consumers together.
The problem it solves
Before a log, systems integrated point to point. Every new consumer of an event meant a new connection into the producer, and each producer carried the delivery concerns of everyone downstream.
Without a logWith a log
N×M integrations — every producer wired to every consumerProducers write once; any number of consumers subscribe independently
Producer knows its consumers, and changes when they changeProducers publish facts and do not know who reads them
A slow consumer applies backpressure to the producer, or drops dataThe log absorbs the difference; the consumer catches up later
No replay — a bug means the data is goneReset the offset and reprocess from retained history
A new consumer needs a backfill projectA new consumer starts at the beginning of retention
Bursts must be handled liveBursts are written to disk and drained at a sustainable rate
Kafka is infrastructure for decoupling, not just messaging. The durability and replay are what let teams evolve producers and consumers on separate schedules — which is usually the real reason it is adopted.

When it fits, and when it does not
Use it forUse something else for
Event streaming and integration between servicesTask queues with per-message ack and retry — a queue is simpler

Kafka is not free to run. Brokers, storage, partition planning, consumer lag monitoring and upgrades are ongoing work. Adopt it when decoupling, replay or throughput genuinely justify that — not because it is the default answer to “we need messaging”.

4  ·  REPLICATION & ISR how durability is actually achieved
The mechanism
  • Each partition has one leader and N−1 followers, where N = replication factor
  • Replication is pull-based — followers issue fetch requests to the leader exactly as consumers do. The leader does not push
  • A follower is in-sync if it has fetched up to the leader within replica.lag.time.max.ms 30000
  • The ISR is the set of in-sync replicas, including the leader. It shrinks and grows dynamically
  • The high watermark advances to the lowest log end offset across the ISR — that is what makes a record readable
This is why acks=all and min.insync.replicas are one mechanism, not two settings. acks=all waits for the ISR; min.insync.replicas defines how small the ISR may become before writes are rejected. Without the second, the first can be satisfied by a single-member ISR.

Leader epoch
Every leadership change increments a monotonic epoch number (KIP-101). Followers use it to determine exactly where their log diverged from the new leader and truncate correctly. It exists because truncating on the high watermark alone could produce divergent logs across replicas during rapid leadership changes.

5  ·  WRITE PATH & READ PATH what actually happens on send() and poll()
Write — producer to acknowledged
1 send() appends to an in-memory batch, per partition — returns immediately with a Future 2 batch closes on batch.size or linger.ms, whichever first 3 compression applied to the whole batch 4 sent to the partition leader only 5 leader appends to its log → OS page cache — not fsync'd per record by default 6 followers fetch and append 7 high watermark advances 8 ack returned per acks setting
Kafka does not fsync every record. Durability comes from replication across brokers, not from flushing to disk on each write. This is deliberate — it is why acks and replication factor matter far more than any flush setting, and why log.flush.interval.messages is best left alone.
Compression is per batch, not per record. So batch.size and linger.ms directly determine the compression ratio you achieve. Fuller batches compress better and cost fewer requests.
Idempotent producer internals
  • Producer is assigned a producer ID and an epoch
  • Each record batch carries a sequence number per partition
  • The broker rejects a duplicate or out-of-order sequence, so a retried batch is written once
  • This is why max.in.flight ≤ 5 is required — the broker tracks a bounded sequence window
Read — poll to record
1 consumer sends a fetch to the partition leader — or a near replica if KIP-392 is configured 2 broker waits for fetch.min.bytes or until fetch.max.wait.ms elapses 3 data is served from the OS page cache when it is recent — sequential, no disk seek 4 batches are returned still compressed — the consumer decompresses 5 consumer reads up to the high watermark — or the LSO under read_committed 6 offsets committed to __consumer_offsets
Zero-copy, and when you lose it. For plaintext connections Kafka can transfer bytes from the page cache to the socket without copying them through the JVM. Enabling TLS removes that optimisation, because the data must be encrypted in user space. TLS is still the right choice in production — but budget for the CPU cost rather than being surprised by it.
The consumer, not the broker, holds the cursor. Nothing in the broker tracks where a given consumer is. That is why replay is trivial — seek to an offset or timestamp and read again — and why an accidental offset reset silently reprocesses or skips data.

6  ·  CONSUMER GROUPS scaling and coordination
The assignment rule
  • Each partition is consumed by exactly one consumer within a group
  • A consumer may hold many partitions; a partition is never split across consumers in the same group
  • Partition count caps parallelism. Consumers beyond the partition count sit idle
  • Different groups consume the same topic independently, each with its own offsets
Who does what during a rebalance
RoleResponsibility
Group coordinator
a broker		Tracks membership and liveness, and stores committed offsets
Group leader
one of the consumers		Computes the partition assignment and returns it via the coordinator
Assignment is computed client-side. The broker coordinates membership but does not decide who gets which partition — the elected consumer does, using the configured assignor. That is why the assignment strategy is a client setting and why all members of a group must agree on it.
Offset storage
Committed offsets are records in __consumer_offsets, a compacted internal topic keyed by (group, topic, partition). Compaction keeps the latest commit per key, so the topic stays bounded no matter how long the group runs.
Delivery semantics follow from commit order
OrderResult
Commit before processingAt-most-once — crash loses the record
Commit after processingAt-least-once — crash reprocesses it
Commit inside a transactionExactly-once within Kafka
Auto-commit gives you neither reliably. It commits on a timer, independent of whether your processing finished — so a crash can lose records the group believes were consumed. Disable it wherever loss is unacceptable and commit explicitly after the work succeeds.

12  ·  GROUP LIVENESS where consumers actually break
Two independent liveness checks
MechanismFails when
session.timeout.ms
background heartbeat thread		Process dies or the network partitions — heartbeats stop
max.poll.interval.ms
the poll loop		Process alive but stuck processing; poll() not called again in time
The classic production failure. Processing slows, one poll cycle exceeds max.poll.interval.ms, the consumer is evicted, the group rebalances, partitions move to another instance that is also slow, and it repeats. The group spends its time rebalancing instead of consuming while lag grows — and no component reports an error.
The fix, in order of preference
  • Reduce max.poll.records so each loop finishes well inside the interval
  • Move slow work off the poll thread to a bounded worker pool, with pause/resume for backpressure
  • Raise max.poll.interval.ms only once you know the real processing-time distribution — it delays detection of genuinely stuck consumers
  • Adopt CooperativeStickyAssignor so rebalances stop being stop-the-world
  • Set group.instance.id so restarts inside the session window skip rebalancing
Eager vs cooperative
ProtocolBehaviour
Eager
Range, RoundRobin		Every consumer revokes all partitions, then reassigns — the whole group stops
Cooperative
CooperativeSticky		Only partitions that must move are revoked; the rest keep consuming
Migrating between protocols needs a documented two-step rolling upgrade — the group must not contain members using incompatible protocols simultaneously. Check the procedure for your client version rather than switching in place.
13  ·  EXACTLY-ONCE scope and cost
LevelGuarantee
At-most-onceCommit before processing. Loss on crash, never duplicates
At-least-once
the common default		Commit after processing. Duplicates on crash, never loss
Idempotent producerRemoves duplicates from producer retries only
TransactionalAtomic across consume → process → produce, including offsets
What a transaction spans
// the atomic unit: output records AND the offsets producer.initTransactions() producer.beginTransaction() producer.send(outputRecord) // 1..n topics producer.sendOffsetsToTransaction( // consumed pos. offsets, consumerGroupMetadata) producer.commitTransaction() // consumers must set isolation.level=read_committed
Committing offsets inside the transaction is the whole mechanism. Output and consumption progress succeed or fail together, closing the gap that makes at-least-once produce duplicates. The coordinator writes commit or abort markers into the affected partitions.
The boundary that gets misunderstood
Exactly-once applies within Kafka. It covers Kafka topics in, processing, and Kafka topics out — atomically. It does not extend to an external database, a payment API, or an email send. Those need idempotency keys on your side.
Costs
  • Extra coordination round trips — throughput falls relative to at-least-once
  • Consumers see records only at commit boundaries, so latency rises with transaction size
  • An open transaction holds the LSO back, stalling read_committed consumers
  • Kafka Streams under exactly_once_v2 commits far more frequently by default, trading throughput for shorter latency
Ask whether you need it. Many pipelines are simpler and cheaper with at-least-once plus a consumer made idempotent on a business key.

PLAINTEXT is the default: no encryption, no authentication. Any client that can reach the port can read and write every topic. Note also that TLS disables the zero-copy path, so budget CPU for it — but do enable it.
ACLs and quotas
  • Producers need Write on the topic; transactional producers also need Write on the TransactionalId
  • Consumers need Read on the topic and on the consumer group
  • producer_byte_rate / consumer_byte_rate / request_percentage throttle rather than reject — a misbehaving client slows down instead of taking the cluster with it
Sizing
# partitions: satisfy both bounds partitions ≥ throughput / per_partition_rate partitions ≥ peak_consumer_instances # storage bytes ≈ rate × avg_size × retention_s × RF ÷ compression_ratio # measure per-partition rates on YOUR hardware
More partitions is not free. Each costs file handles, memory on brokers and clients, and controller work. Over-partitioning raises latency and lengthens recovery after a broker failure. TUNE
Alert on lag trend, not a fixed threshold. Steady lag under load is normal; monotonically growing lag means consumption is losing to production and will not recover on its own.

The test that validates the whole sheet. Take one broker out under production-like load.
