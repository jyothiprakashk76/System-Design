WHAT SYSTEM DESIGN IS, AND WHY LEARN ITstart here — the definition, why it matters, and how to use this sheet
The definition

System design is deciding how the parts of a software system fit together so that it meets its requirements at the scale, latency and reliability it actually needs — and keeps meeting them as those change.

It is the layer above writing code and below business strategy: what components exist, how they communicate, where state lives, what happens when a part fails, and what it costs.

The distinguishing skill is not knowing components. It is choosing between them under constraints you were given, and being able to say what each choice costs. Anyone can name a cache; the work is knowing when adding one makes things worse.
What a design has to answer
  • What are the functional and non-functional requirements, stated in numbers?
  • What is the data model, and what are the access patterns?
  • Where is the bottleneck, and what happens when it is reached?
  • What fails, how is it detected, and what does the user see?
  • What does it cost, and how does that scale?
  • What would you revisit, and on what signal?
Why learn it
  • It is what seniority is measured by. Beyond a certain level, the question stops being “can you implement this?” and becomes “should we build it this way?”
  • The expensive mistakes are architectural. A slow function is an afternoon; the wrong partition key, the wrong consistency model or the wrong service boundary is a year.
  • It is the common language between engineering, product and operations for discussing trade-offs.
  • It transfers. Frameworks and cloud services change constantly; queueing, consistency, caching and failure behaviour do not.
  • It is the interview. For most senior roles the system design round carries more weight than the coding one.
The most common way people study this badly is memorising reference architectures for well-known products. Interviewers and real systems both change one constraint and watch what happens. Learn the reasoning, and the architectures follow; learn the architectures, and nothing follows.
The habit worth building first

For every technique on this sheet, learn it as a pair: what it buys and what it costs. Sharding buys write throughput and costs joins and transactions. Caching buys latency and costs correctness. Microservices buy independent deployment and cost you a distributed system to debug.

How long this takes

Theme A is a few weeks of focused study. The rest is measured in years, because most of it only lands once you have operated something that failed in that particular way. That is normal and not a reason to defer starting.

Where to begin if you are unsure. Take one system you already work on. Draw it. Then answer the six questions on the left about it. The branches where you cannot answer are your reading list, in priority order — which is more useful than any generic sequence.
A note on the interview framing. These topics are heavily associated with hiring, and that is a legitimate reason to learn them. But the reason they are asked is that they predict the work — designing under constraint, and knowing what you gave up.

1FUNDAMENTALS & GOALS
The five properties
  • Scalability — handles growth in load, data or users
  • Availability — fraction of time it serves requests
  • Reliability — serves them correctly; not the same thing
  • Performance — latency and throughput under load
  • Maintainability — the one that decides your velocity in year two
Availability, as a number
  • 99% ≈ 3.65 days of downtime a year
  • 99.9% ≈ 8.8 hours  ·  99.99% ≈ 53 minutes
  • 99.999% ≈ 5 minutes — very expensive, rarely required
  • Serial dependencies multiply: five 99.9% services in a chain give ≈ 99.5%
Core reading
  • Latency vs throughput vs concurrency
  • Little's Law — L = λW
  • Why adding servers stops helping (contention and coherency)
Availability ≠ reliability. A service that returns wrong answers quickly is highly available and completely unreliable. Interviews and incident reviews both punish conflating them.
2REQUIREMENTS & ESTIMATION
Clarify before designing
  • Functional requirements — what it must do
  • Non-functional — scale, latency target, consistency, availability, retention, budget
  • Explicit out-of-scope, so the design has an edge
  • Who the users are, and the read:write ratio
Back-of-the-envelope
  • DAU / MAU → requests per second, peak vs average
  • Peak is typically several times the mean — design for peak
  • Storage per record × records × replication × retention
  • Bandwidth, connection count, memory for cache working set
  • Powers of two, and rough latency numbers (memory vs SSD vs network vs cross-region)
Percentiles, not averages
  • p50, p95, p99, p99.9 — the tail is what users feel
  • Fan-out amplifies tails: 100 parallel calls at p99 = 1s means most requests hit one
  • Averages hide bimodal behaviour completely
State assumptions out loud and write them down. An estimate with a stated assumption is engineering; a number with no assumption is a guess that someone will later treat as a requirement.
3NETWORKING & PROTOCOLS
Foundations
  • TCP vs UDP — ordering and delivery vs latency
  • DNS resolution, TTL, and why deploys wait on it
  • TLS handshake cost, session resumption, mTLS
  • Latency budget: RTT, bandwidth-delay product, geography
HTTP versions
  • HTTP/1.1 — one request per connection at a time, head-of-line blocking
  • HTTP/2 — multiplexed streams over one TCP connection; still TCP-level HOL blocking
  • HTTP/3 / QUIC — over UDP, removes transport HOL blocking, faster connection setup
Real-time and streaming
  • Short polling → long polling → SSE → WebSocket
  • Pick by direction: SSE is server→client only; WebSocket is bidirectional
  • Connection state is the cost — WebSockets make your servers stateful
  • gRPC / HTTP/2 for internal service-to-service
Absent from most roadmaps, and asked constantly. “How does the client learn about a new message?” is a protocol question, and polling, SSE and WebSockets give three different systems.
4API DESIGN
Styles, and when each fits
  • REST — resource-oriented, cacheable, ubiquitous. The default for public APIs
  • GraphQL — client picks the shape; solves over-fetching, costs you caching and query-cost control
  • gRPC — binary, typed contracts, streaming. Strong for internal calls, awkward from browsers
  • Webhooks — server-to-server push; needs retries, signing and idempotency
Contract concerns
  • Versioning — path, header, or never break; pick one and hold it
  • Pagination — cursor over offset at scale
  • Idempotency keys on every unsafe retryable operation
  • Consistent error envelope and error codes
  • Rate limit headers, and what a 429 should tell the caller
  • Filtering, sorting, partial responses, bulk endpoints
Backward compatibility is the whole discipline. Adding a field is safe; removing one, renaming one, or tightening validation is a breaking change even when the compiler stays quiet.
5DATA STORES BY FAMILY
Families and representatives
  • Relational — PostgreSQL, MySQL, Oracle, SQL Server
  • Document — MongoDB, Couchbase, DocumentDB
  • Key-value — Redis, DynamoDB, Riak
  • Wide-column — Cassandra, ScyllaDB, HBase, Bigtable
  • Columnar / analytics — ClickHouse, Snowflake, BigQuery, Druid
  • Graph — Neo4j, Amazon Neptune, JanusGraph
  • Time series — InfluxDB, TimescaleDB, Prometheus
  • Search — Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Solr
  • NewSQL / distributed SQL — CockroachDB, TiDB, YugabyteDB, Spanner
  • Vector — pgvector, Pinecone, Weaviate, Milvus, Qdrant
  • Object storage — S3, GCS, Azure Blob
  • Embedded — SQLite, DuckDB, RocksDB
Vector stores are now a standard family, not a niche. Any system with semantic search, recommendations or retrieval-augmented generation needs one — and pgvector often removes the need for a separate service entirely.
Start relational. Postgres handles documents, key-value, full-text, geospatial and vectors competently. Adding a second store adds consistency, operations and failure modes — earn it with a measured reason, not an anticipated one.
6STORAGE ENGINES & INDEXING
The two engine families
  • B-tree — update in place. Predictable reads, slower random writes. Postgres, MySQL/InnoDB
  • LSM tree — append to a memtable, flush to sorted files, compact in background. Fast writes, read amplification, compaction cost. Cassandra, RocksDB, ScyllaDB
  • Read / write / space amplification — the three-way trade every engine picks a point on
Indexing
  • Primary vs secondary; clustered vs non-clustered
  • Composite indexes and why column order decides usability
  • Covering indexes — answer from the index, never touch the row
  • Partial and functional indexes
  • Inverted index for search; geospatial and vector indexes
  • Every index speeds reads and slows every write
This branch explains the rest of the data section. “Why is Cassandra fast at writes?” and “why does this query need a full scan?” are both storage-engine questions, and neither is answerable from a list of database names.
7CACHING
Patterns
  • Cache-aside — app reads cache, misses, loads, populates. The default
  • Read-through — cache loads on miss itself
  • Write-through — write both, consistent, slower writes
  • Write-behind — write cache, flush async. Fast, risks loss
  • Refresh-ahead — refresh hot keys before expiry
Eviction & invalidation
  • LRU, LFU, FIFO, TTL, and random
  • Invalidation is the hard half — TTL, explicit, or event-driven
  • Versioned keys avoid deletion races
Failure modes
  • Stampede / thundering herd — many misses at once on expiry. Fix with jittered TTL, locks or early recompute
  • Hot key — one key saturates one node
  • Penetration — repeated misses for keys that do not exist; cache the negative
  • Cold start after a restart or deploy
Where caches live
  • Browser → CDN → gateway → application → distributed cache → database buffer pool
Deserves a full branch, not a footnote. Caching is the most commonly deployed and most commonly mis-configured technique in the whole roadmap — and every entry above is a real production incident class.
8DISTRIBUTED FUNDAMENTALS
Theory you will actually use
  • CAP — under a partition, choose consistency or availability. Not a permanent choice of two from three
  • PACELC — and when there is no partition, latency vs consistency. The more useful framing day to day
  • ACID vs BASE
  • Consistency models — strong, sequential, causal, eventual, read-your-writes, monotonic reads
What actually goes wrong
  • Partial failure — the defining property of a distributed system
  • Network is unreliable, latency non-zero, topology changes — the eight fallacies
  • Timeouts, retries, and the retry storms they cause
  • Idempotency — the only thing that makes retries safe
  • Exactly-once delivery does not exist; at-least-once + idempotency does
  • Clock skew — never order events by wall clock across machines
  • Replication lag, stale reads, split-brain
  • Backpressure and head-of-line blocking
The single most useful sentence here: a timeout tells you nothing about whether the other side did the work. Every correctness argument in a distributed system starts from that.
9CONSENSUS & COORDINATION
Agreement
  • Raft — leader election, log replication, safety. Learn this one properly
  • Paxos / Multi-Paxos — the ancestor; know the shape
  • ZAB (ZooKeeper), and where etcd and Consul fit
  • Why consensus needs a majority, and why even-sized clusters are a mistake
Quorums
  • R + W > N gives read-your-writes on a quorum store
  • Tunable consistency in Cassandra and DynamoDB
  • Hinted handoff, read repair, anti-entropy
Ordering & coordination
  • Lamport clocks, vector clocks, version vectors
  • Leader election and fencing tokens
  • Distributed locks — and why lock + timeout is not mutual exclusion
  • Failure detection: heartbeats, gossip, phi-accrual
  • CRDTs for conflict-free merges
A conspicuous gap in most roadmaps. “How does the cluster agree on a leader?” and “what happens when the leader is only partially unreachable?” are the questions that separate familiarity from understanding.
10SCALING & DISTRIBUTION
Directions
  • Vertical — simple, bounded, no distribution problems. Do this first
  • Horizontal — unbounded, and buys you every problem in theme C
  • Stateless services scale trivially; state is the actual problem
Replication
  • Leader–follower (formerly master–slave) — one writer, many readers
  • Multi-leader — write anywhere, conflict resolution required
  • Leaderless — quorum reads and writes
  • Synchronous vs asynchronous, and the replication lag it creates
  • Failover, promotion, and split-brain protection
Other levers
  • Read replicas, CQRS-style read models, denormalisation
  • CDN and edge for static and cacheable content
  • Autoscaling — and why it never saves you from a cold cache
  • Multi-region: active-passive vs active-active, data residency
  • Cell-based architecture and shuffle sharding to bound blast radius
Prefer leader–follower / primary–replica terminology. It is both the industry standard now and more precise about what the roles do.
11PARTITIONING & CONSISTENT HASHING
Partitioning strategies
  • Range — ordered scans work; hotspots on sequential keys
  • Hash — even spread; range queries stop working
  • Directory — flexible, adds a lookup and a dependency
  • Composite keys: partition key + sort key
Consistent hashing
  • The hash ring, and why plain hash % N remaps almost everything on resize
  • Virtual nodes — many points per physical node, for even distribution and smooth rebalancing
  • Adding and removing a node moves only that node's share
  • Used by Cassandra, DynamoDB, Memcached clients, and load balancers for sticky routing
  • Rendezvous hashing as a simpler alternative
What goes wrong
  • Hot partition / celebrity key — one key or tenant dominates. Salt it, or split it
  • Skew from a poorly chosen partition key is the most common scaling defect
  • Resharding a live system is difficult — choose the key as if you cannot change it
  • Cross-partition joins and transactions are what you gave up
12LOAD BALANCING, PROXIES & GATEWAYS
Three roles, often confused
  • Reverse proxy — sits in front of servers: TLS termination, compression, caching, static content. NGINX, HAProxy, Traefik, Envoy
  • API gateway — a reverse proxy plus API concerns: authN/authZ, rate limiting, quotas, request transformation, protocol translation, aggregation, per-consumer metering. Kong, Apigee, AWS API Gateway, Tyk
  • Service mesh — proxies beside every service for east-west traffic: mTLS, retries, circuit breaking, traffic splitting, telemetry. Istio, Linkerd
  • Forward proxy — sits in front of clients; controls egress
Load balancing
  • L4 — transport level, fast, protocol-blind. L7 — understands HTTP, can route on path, header, cookie
  • Algorithms: round robin, weighted, least connections, least response time, consistent hashing
  • Health checks, connection draining, sticky sessions and why to avoid them
  • Global load balancing: DNS, anycast, GeoDNS
North-south vs east-west. Gateways handle traffic entering the system; a mesh handles traffic between services. Choosing between them is a category error — most large systems run both.
13RESILIENCE PATTERNS
Stop a failure spreading
  • Timeout — bound every remote call, always. Nothing else works without it
  • Retry with exponential backoff + jitter — jitter is not optional; without it every client retries in lockstep
  • Circuit breaker — stop calling a dependency that is already failing
  • Bulkhead — isolate pools so one dependency cannot exhaust all threads or connections
  • Rate limiting & throttling — protect the callee
  • Load shedding — reject early under overload rather than degrading for everyone
  • Graceful degradation — serve less rather than nothing
  • Fallback — cached, default, or queued; never a second remote call
  • Idempotency — what makes every retry above safe
  • Health checks — liveness must not fail because a dependency is down
These are not GoF design patterns and should not be listed beside Singleton and Factory. Those describe object construction and collaboration inside one process; these describe how a system behaves when a remote dependency fails. Different problem, different scale, different chapter.
14ARCHITECTURAL STYLES
System-level styles
  • Monolith — one deployable. Simplest, and correct far more often than its reputation suggests
  • Modular monolith — enforced internal boundaries, one deployment. Usually the right next step
  • Microservices — independent deployment and scaling, at the cost of a distributed system
  • Service-oriented, event-driven, serverless
  • Layered, hexagonal / ports and adapters, clean architecture
  • CQRS — separate read and write models
  • Pipes and filters, actor model, cell-based
Supporting patterns
  • BFF — a backend per client type
  • Sidecar / ambassador
  • Strangler fig — the standard way to replace a legacy system incrementally
  • API composition and service discovery
  • Database per service, and the joins you lose
Conway's Law is a design constraint, not a joke. Systems reflect the communication structure of the teams that build them, so a service boundary that cuts across a team boundary will be fought continuously until one of them moves.
15DISTRIBUTED TRANSACTIONS
Local first
  • Isolation levels: read uncommitted → read committed → repeatable read → serializable
  • The anomalies each one permits: dirty read, non-repeatable read, phantom, write skew
  • Optimistic (version column) vs pessimistic (locking) concurrency
  • MVCC — how most modern databases avoid readers blocking writers
Across services
  • 2PC — atomic, blocking, and a coordinator you must keep alive. Rare in modern designs
  • 3PC — reduces blocking, adds complexity, still assumes a lot
  • Saga — a sequence of local transactions with compensating actions. Choreography (events) or orchestration (a coordinator)
  • TCC — try, confirm, cancel with explicit reservation
  • Outbox — write state and event in one local transaction; publish from the outbox
  • Inbox / idempotent consumer — deduplicate on receipt
  • CDC — read the log rather than dual-writing
Sagas give up isolation, not just atomicity. Intermediate states are visible to other readers, so “the order exists but is unpaid” must be a state your domain model actually has — not a window you hope nobody looks into.
16MESSAGING & EVENT STREAMING
Queue vs log — the distinction that matters
  • Queue (RabbitMQ, SQS, ActiveMQ) — a message is delivered, acknowledged and removed. Competing consumers share the work. Per-message acks, visibility timeouts, DLQs
  • Log (Kafka, Pulsar) — records are appended and retained; consumers track an offset. Many independent consumers read the same data, and replay is a normal operation
  • Choosing wrongly here shapes everything downstream
Mechanics
  • Topics, partitions, consumer groups, offsets
  • Ordering is per partition, never global
  • At-most-once / at-least-once / effectively-once
  • DLQ, poison messages, retry topics
  • Backpressure, lag monitoring, rebalancing
  • Fan-out, competing consumers, request-reply
Event-driven design
  • Event sourcing, event replay, materialized views
  • Schema registry and versioning (Avro, Protobuf) — events are a contract
  • Event-carried state transfer vs thin events
  • Stream processing: windowing, watermarks, joins — Flink, Kafka Streams
17IAM, AUTHN & AUTHZ
Two different questions
  • Authentication — who is this? Authorization — what may they do? Never one control
Protocols & tokens
  • OAuth 2.0 / 2.1 — delegated authorization; grant types and which are still safe
  • OIDC — authentication layered on OAuth; ID tokens
  • SAML — enterprise federation, still everywhere
  • JWT — structure, signing, validation, expiry, and why revocation is hard
  • Session cookies vs bearer tokens; refresh token rotation
  • Token exchange for on-behalf-of calls between services
  • API keys and HMAC request signing
Authorization models
  • RBAC roles · ABAC attributes · ReBAC relationships (Zanzibar-style)
  • Permission-based checks, not role-name checks
  • Multi-tenancy and tenant isolation tests
Service-to-service & operations
  • mTLS and workload identity — network position is not authentication
  • Secrets management and rotation (Vault, cloud KMS)
  • MFA, account recovery, breach response
  • Providers: Keycloak, Auth0, Okta, Cognito, Microsoft Entra ID, Google Identity
18OBSERVABILITY & OPERATIONS
The three signals
  • Logs — structured, correlated, sampled. ELK, Loki
  • Metrics — cheap, aggregatable, low cardinality. Prometheus, Grafana
  • Traces — one request across services. Jaeger, Tempo, OpenTelemetry
  • Correlation and trace IDs propagated through every hop, including async ones
  • OpenTelemetry as the vendor-neutral instrumentation standard
Knowing what to measure
  • RED — rate, errors, duration (services)
  • USE — utilisation, saturation, errors (resources)
  • Four golden signals: latency, traffic, errors, saturation
  • SLI → SLO → SLA, and error budgets that actually gate releases
  • Cardinality discipline — a user ID as a metric label will bankrupt you
Operating it
  • Alert on symptoms, not causes; page only on user impact
  • Runbooks, on-call rotation, incident command
  • Blameless postmortems and action tracking
  • Chaos engineering and game days
  • Capacity planning and load testing
19ALGORITHMS FOR SYSTEM DESIGN
Probabilistic structures
  • Bloom filter — “definitely not present” in constant space. Used before every expensive lookup
  • HyperLogLog — approximate distinct counts at tiny memory cost
  • Count-Min Sketch — approximate frequencies, heavy hitters
  • Skip lists, tries, LSM merge, consistent hash rings
Rate limiting algorithms
  • Fixed window · sliding window log · sliding window counter
  • Token bucket (allows bursts) · leaky bucket (smooths output)
  • Distributed rate limiting and its coordination cost
Identifiers
  • UUID v4 vs v7, Snowflake IDs, ULID, KSUID
  • Sortable vs random, and what random keys do to B-tree insert locality
Geospatial & search
  • Geohash, quadtree, S2, R-tree — proximity search
  • Inverted index, TF-IDF, BM25; vector similarity and ANN
  • Top-K, sampling, reservoir sampling
These turn up in almost every design problem. Nearby drivers is geospatial indexing; unique visitors is HyperLogLog; “have we seen this ID” is a Bloom filter.
20DELIVERY, DR & COST
Getting changes out safely
  • CI/CD pipelines, trunk-based development
  • Deployment strategies: rolling, blue-green, canary, shadow traffic
  • Feature flags — decouple deploy from release
  • Backward-compatible migrations: expand → migrate → contract
  • Rollback that is tested, not assumed
Infrastructure
  • Containers, orchestration, autoscaling
  • Infrastructure as code, immutable infrastructure
  • Environment parity and configuration management
Continuity
  • RTO — how long you may be down. RPO — how much data you may lose
  • Backups, and restores that are actually rehearsed
  • Multi-AZ vs multi-region; failover drills
  • Data residency and retention obligations
Cost
  • Cost per request, per tenant, per feature
  • Egress, storage tiers, idle capacity
  • Cost as a design constraint, stated alongside latency
Usually the last branch learned and the first one that bites. A design that cannot be deployed safely, restored, or afforded is not finished.
GENERAL DESIGN PRINCIPLES
  • KISS — the simplest thing that meets the requirement
  • YAGNI — build for today's requirement, not an imagined one
  • DRY — but duplication is cheaper than the wrong abstraction
  • Loose coupling, high cohesion
  • Separation of concerns and single responsibility
  • Design for failure — assume every dependency will fail
  • Make it work, make it right, make it fast — in that order
  • Principle of least privilege
  • Idempotency by default for anything retryable
  • Conway's Law — architecture follows team structure
  • Think in trade-offs — name the cost, not only the benefit
Premature optimisation and premature distribution are the same mistake. Both add permanent complexity to buy a benefit you have not yet measured a need for.
A FRAMEWORK FOR DESIGN PROBLEMS
  • 1 · Clarify — functional and non-functional requirements, scale, out-of-scope. Ask before drawing
  • 2 · Estimate — QPS, storage, bandwidth. State assumptions aloud
  • 3 · Define the API — the contract forces the data model into the open
  • 4 · Data model — entities, access patterns, then the store that fits them
  • 5 · High-level design — boxes and arrows, happy path only
  • 6 · Deep dive — take the one or two components that carry the risk
  • 7 · Scale it — find the bottleneck, apply one technique, name what it costs
  • 8 · Failure & operations — what breaks, what degrades, what you monitor
  • 9 · Trade-offs — state what you would revisit and under what signal
Access patterns before storage choice. Deciding the database first and then bending the queries to fit it is the most common way a design goes wrong in the first five minutes.

Group by pattern, not by product. Ticket booking and inventory reservation are the same concurrency problem; feed and leaderboard are the same fan-out and ranking problem. Learning the pattern transfers; memorising the answer does not.
SUPPORTING GROUNDWORK

Not part of the roadmap itself, but every branch above assumes it.

  • Computer networks — TCP/IP, DNS, HTTP, TLS, NAT, routing
  • Operating systems — processes and threads, context switching, memory, file I/O, epoll
  • Data structures & algorithms — hash maps, trees, heaps, graphs, complexity
  • Databases — SQL, query planning, transactions, normalisation

Operating systems and networking pay the highest dividend. Most senior system design reasoning — connection limits, thread pools, buffering, latency budgets, backpressure — is applied OS and networking knowledge wearing different vocabulary.
