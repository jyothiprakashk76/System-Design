1  ·  WHAT A PAYMENT GATEWAY IS, WHO ELSE IS INVOLVED, AND WHY THIS IS HARD start here — the participants, the vocabulary, and the three properties that make payments unlike any other system
The definition, and what it is not
A payment gateway captures payment credentials securely, turns them into an authorisation request, routes it to whoever can approve it, and reports the outcome. It is the merchant's entry point to the payments network — not the entity that holds or moves the money.
PartyRole
CardholderThe customer paying
MerchantSells the goods; holds a merchant account
GatewayCaptures credentials, routes the request, returns the result
Processor / PSPHandles the transaction mechanics with the networks. Often bundled with the gateway commercially, but a distinct function
AcquirerThe merchant's bank. Holds the merchant account, receives the funds, carries the risk
Card networkVisa, Mastercard, RuPay — routes between acquirer and issuer, sets the rules and the fees
IssuerThe cardholder's bank. Makes the approve-or-decline decision and bills the customer
This is the four-party model — cardholder, acquirer, network, issuer — and almost every card payment in the world runs on it. Being able to name who actually decides (the issuer) and who actually bears the risk (the acquirer) is the fastest way to show you understand the domain.
Vocabulary the rest of the sheet uses
AuthorisationThe issuer checks funds and risk, then holds the amount. No money has moved
CaptureThe merchant claims the held amount. Usually at dispatch, not at checkout
Void / reversalCancelling an authorisation before capture. Cheap and clean
ClearingBatch exchange of transaction detail between acquirer and issuer
SettlementThe actual movement of funds, typically T+1 or T+2
RefundReturning money after capture. A new transaction, not an undo
ChargebackThe cardholder disputes through their issuer. Forcible reversal, plus a fee
PANThe card number. Never store it
TokenA reference standing in for the PAN
MDR / interchangeWhat the merchant pays; interchange is the issuer's share of it
The distinction that trips people up. Authorisation is not payment. An approved authorisation is a promise the issuer will honour a claim if made within a window — typically days. Capture is when you claim it. Settlement is when the funds actually arrive. Three different events, three different days.
Why this is harder than it looks
PropertyConsequence
Errors are not reversible by a deployA double charge is a customer's money and a regulatory matter. There is no rollback — only a compensating refund, and the damage to trust is already done
Every dependency is externalIssuer, network and acquirer are outside your control, on their maintenance windows, with their rate limits and their outages
Timeouts are ambiguousA timeout tells you nothing about whether the customer was charged. This single fact drives most of the design
Correctness is auditedEvery rupee must reconcile against the provider and the bank, daily, with an explanation for every break
The rules are regulatedPCI DSS, strong authentication, tokenisation mandates, data residency. Non-compliance is a business risk, not a technical one
The three design commitments everything else follows from. 1 · Every operation that moves money is idempotent, keyed by the caller. 2 · The ledger is append-only and double-entry, so state is derived rather than overwritten. 3 · Nothing is believed until it reconciles against an external source of truth.
Availability target, stated honestly. 99.99% for the API you control, but the end-to-end success rate depends on issuers you do not. Design for graceful failure and clear messaging rather than for a number you cannot deliver alone.

3  ·  THE PAYMENT STATE MACHINE including the state people omit
CREATED │ ├─▶ AUTHORISED ──▶ CAPTURED ──▶ SETTLED │ │ │ │ │ ├─▶ REFUNDED │ │ ├─▶ PARTIALLY_REFUNDED │ │ └─▶ DISPUTED ─▶ CHARGEBACK_LOST │ │ └▶ CHARGEBACK_WON │ ├─▶ VOIDED // cancelled pre-capture │ └─▶ AUTH_EXPIRED // hold lapsed, not captured │ ├─▶ DECLINED // issuer said no — a normal outcome ├─▶ FAILED // our side or a validation error │ └─▶ UNKNOWN ◀── timeout / no response │ └─▶ resolved by query or reconciliation into one of the states above
UNKNOWN is the state most designs are missing, and it is the one that causes double charges. It is not a failure and must never be treated as one. A payment in this state has an unresolved external truth, and the only valid actions are query the provider or wait for reconciliation — never retry, and never tell the customer it failed.
Rules for the machine
  • Transitions are append-only events, not column updates. The history is what an auditor asks for
  • Only forward transitions are legal. A CAPTURED payment never returns to AUTHORISED
  • Out-of-order webhooks are normal — ignore any transition older than current state
  • Every terminal state has an owner and a defined customer-facing message
  • Alert on anything stuck beyond its expected window — this is how stranded payments are found before customers find them
AUTH_EXPIRED is worth modelling explicitly. Authorisation holds lapse after a period set by the network and issuer. If you capture late and the hold has gone, the capture fails and the customer may already have the goods. Track the expiry and act before it.
4  ·  IDEMPOTENCY AND THE AMBIGUOUS TIMEOUT the single most important mechanism on this sheet — everything that moves money depends on it
The governing fact, stated once. When a payment call times out, four things are equally possible: the request never arrived; it arrived and was declined; it arrived and succeeded but the response was lost; or it is still being processed. Your system cannot distinguish them. Every mechanism below exists because of that.
The idempotency contract
// caller generates the key when intent is formed, // and reuses it for every retry of the SAME intent POST /v1/payments Idempotency-Key: 01J8F3K2M9Q // server, in one transaction: INSERT INTO idempotency(key, request_hash, state) VALUES (?, ?, 'IN_PROGRESS') ON CONFLICT (key) DO NOTHING // 0 rows → this key was seen before ├─ completed? → return the ORIGINAL response ├─ in progress? → return 409, tell caller to poll └─ new? → process, store the result
Return the original response, not an error. A repeat with the same key must return the first result. Returning a conflict makes the caller believe it failed — and they will retry again, which is precisely the loop you are trying to prevent.
Bind the key to the request body. Store a hash of the payload; if the same key arrives with a different amount, reject it. Otherwise a client bug can turn one key into a way of overwriting an earlier payment's identity.
Retry policy — the rules that prevent double charges
SituationCorrect action
Connection refused
request provably never landed		Safe to retry with the same key
Timeout after sendDo not retry blindly. Retry only with the same idempotency key, or query the provider for the status of that key
HTTP 5xxRetry with the same key, with exponential backoff and jitter
HTTP 4xxDo not retry. It is deterministic and will fail identically
Explicit declineDo not retry automatically. A different card or method is a customer decision
The three-layer defence. 1 · Your idempotency key stops your own retries duplicating. 2 · The PSP's idempotency key stops your calls duplicating at their end — pass one, always. 3 · Daily reconciliation catches anything that slipped through both. Any one layer alone is insufficient.
Key lifecycle, which is usually forgotten
  • Generated at intent, in the client or at the start of the flow — never at the retry site, which would produce a fresh key each attempt and defeat the mechanism entirely
  • Carried end to end, including to the PSP
  • Retained longer than the longest plausible retry window — days, not minutes. Delete too early and a late retry is processed as new
  • Scoped per merchant, so one merchant's keys cannot collide with another's
Exactly-once does not exist across systems. What you build is at-least-once delivery with idempotent processing, which produces the same observable outcome — and saying so precisely is a stronger answer than claiming a guarantee that is not achievable.
5  ·  THE LEDGER — DOUBLE-ENTRY, IMMUTABLE, AND WHY MONEY IS AN INTEGER how a payment system proves it is right, rather than asserting it
Double-entry, in one paragraph
Every movement is recorded as balanced entries across two or more accounts. Debits equal credits, always. A balance is not a stored number that gets updated — it is the sum of the entries. That is what makes the system auditable and self-checking.
// customer pays ₹2,499.00 — captured journal_entry: je_8831 ts: 2026-09-01T10:14:22Z DEBIT merchant_receivable 249900 CREDIT customer_payable 249900 // gateway fee 2% recognised at capture journal_entry: je_8832 DEBIT merchant_receivable 4998 CREDIT fee_revenue 4998 // settlement lands T+1, net of fees journal_entry: je_9104 DEBIT bank_settlement 244902 CREDIT merchant_receivable 244902 // invariant, checked continuously: SUM(debits) == SUM(credits) — always, no exceptions
Why this is worth the effort. When a figure is disputed, you can show every entry that produced it and when it arrived. An overwritten balance column can only tell you what it says now — which is exactly the wrong answer to give an auditor or a regulator.
Money representation — non-negotiable rules
Integer minor units₹2,499.00 is stored as 249900. Never a float — binary floating point cannot represent 0.1 exactly, and rounding errors in money are real losses that accumulate
Always with a currencyAn amount without a currency code is meaningless. Store them together, as one value object
Minor units differTwo decimal places is not universal — some currencies have none, some have three. Do not hard-code 100
Rounding is a policyDecide it once, write it down, apply it consistently, and record which entry absorbed the remainder when splitting
FX is a dated factStore the rate and the timestamp used with the transaction. Recomputing later with today's rate produces a different answer and an unexplainable break
Design properties that follow
  • Append-only. No UPDATE, no DELETE on journal entries. A correction is a new reversing entry, so the mistake and the fix are both visible
  • Idempotent by construction — a journal entry carries the idempotency key that produced it, so replay cannot double-post
  • CP, single-writer per account — this is exactly where strong consistency is worth its cost
  • Partition by time, and keep the history. It will be asked for
  • Continuous invariant checks — debits equal credits, no negative balances where prohibited, no orphan entries. Alert on violation immediately
Do not put the ledger in an eventually-consistent store. Redis, a search index or a replica are all fine for reading balances on a dashboard. The authoritative entries belong in a transactional database with real isolation — this is the clearest case on any of these sheets for choosing CP.
6  ·  PCI SCOPE & TOKENISATION designing the problem away
PCI DSS applies to any system that stores, processes or transmits cardholder data. The winning strategy is not to comply harder — it is to arrange the architecture so almost nothing is in scope.
How scope is minimised
ApproachEffect
Hosted fields / iframeThe card input is served by the provider; the browser posts the PAN directly to them. The merchant server never receives it, qualifying for the lightest assessment
Full redirectSimplest scope of all, at the cost of losing control of the checkout experience
Direct APICard data crosses your servers. Full assessment, annual audit, network segmentation. Only worth it if you are the processor
Client-side scripts are now the attack surface that matters. Recent PCI guidance places specific emphasis on authorising and monitoring scripts on the payment page, because skimming attacks target the browser rather than the server. An iframe helps, but a compromised parent page is still a risk — inventory your scripts and enforce integrity.
Never stored, under any circumstances
  • CVV / CVC after authorisation — prohibited outright, no exceptions for convenience
  • Full PAN outside a vault, and never in logs, traces, error messages, analytics or support tickets
  • Track or chip data post-authorisation
What you may safely keep: the token, last four digits, brand, expiry and BIN range. That is sufficient for saved cards, receipts, routing and support — every customer-facing feature people ask for.
Redaction must be enforced, not requested. Card numbers reach logs through error objects, request dumps and third-party SDKs. Filter at the logging library and scan for PAN patterns in your log pipeline — a convention that relies on developers remembering will fail.
7  ·  AUTHENTICATION & FRAUD the trade-off nobody escapes
3-D Secure — what it actually does
The issuer authenticates the cardholder before authorisation. Modern implementations pass rich device and transaction data so most sessions are frictionless — approved with no customer interaction — and only riskier ones are challenged.
The commercial point is liability. A successfully authenticated transaction generally shifts fraud liability from the merchant to the issuer. That is why merchants adopt it even though every added step costs conversion.
The tension, stated honestly
More frictionLess fraud, more liability protection, and measurably fewer completed checkouts
Less frictionHigher conversion, more fraud losses and more chargebacks
Route by risk, not by rule. Challenge the risky minority and let the rest through. A blanket policy either loses revenue on safe transactions or absorbs fraud on dangerous ones — and where regulation mandates strong authentication, exemptions usually exist for low-value or low-risk cases and are worth using.
Fraud signals worth having
  • Velocity — attempts per card, per device, per IP, per address in a window
  • Device fingerprint and session behaviour
  • BIN intelligence — issuer country versus billing and shipping country
  • Mismatch signals — billing/shipping distance, new address on a first order
  • Card testing — many small authorisations across many cards from one source
The cost of a false positive is invisible and larger than it looks. A declined genuine customer rarely complains — they leave. Track the decline rate as carefully as the fraud rate, and sample declined transactions for review, or the model will quietly optimise itself into blocking real revenue.
8  ·  ROUTING & RESILIENCE every dependency is someone else's
Once volume justifies more than one acquirer or PSP, which route a transaction takes becomes a design decision — and one with direct revenue consequences.
Why route at all
Authorisation rateThe same card can be approved by one acquirer and declined by another. Small percentage differences are large revenue differences
CostFees vary by route, card type and geography
RedundancyProviders have outages. A single integration means their downtime is your downtime
Local acquiringDomestic routes usually approve better and cost less than cross-border ones
Method coverageCards, bank transfers, wallets and real-time rails rarely come from one provider
Making it safe
  • Health-aware routing — track success rate and latency per route and shift traffic away from a degrading one automatically
  • Circuit breaker per provider, so a failing route fails fast instead of consuming your capacity
  • Cascading retry with care — retrying a declined transaction on another route is sometimes legitimate and sometimes prohibited by scheme rules. Know which, and never cascade on a timeout
  • Provider-specific idempotency keys so a cascade cannot double-charge
  • An abstraction layer so adding a provider is configuration, not a rewrite — but expect their semantics to differ more than their APIs suggest
Cascading on an ambiguous outcome is how double charges happen at scale. If route A timed out, you do not know it failed. Resolve A's status first. Only a definitive decline is safe to re-route, and even then only where the scheme permits it.
Measure authorisation rate per route, per BIN, per hour. It is the single most valuable metric in a payments business, and it moves without anything in your code changing.

Cost: single-writer scaling. Alternative: distributed SQL when write volume genuinely exceeds one node

Non-negotiable for key material
The honest summary. A payment system is not technically exotic — it is a transactional database, a queue and a great deal of discipline. The difficulty is in the invariants, the failure handling and the reconciliation, none of which a technology choice solves for you.
10  ·  RECONCILIATION, SETTLEMENT AND DISPUTES the unglamorous half, where correctness is proved and where most payment engineering time actually goes
Three-way reconciliation, daily
your ledger ⟷ PSP report ⟷ bank statement (what you think) (what they say) (what arrived) // every transaction must appear in all three, // with the same amount, on the same date
Break typeUsual cause
In ours, not theirsAn UNKNOWN we optimistically marked successful. The dangerous one
In theirs, not oursA response we lost — the customer was charged and we do not know
Amount mismatchFees, FX rounding, partial capture
Date mismatchTime zone or cut-off boundary, not a real break
DuplicateAn idempotency failure. Investigate the root cause, never just net it off
Reconciliation is not a report — it is a control. Every break needs an owner, an age and a resolution. Unexplained breaks accumulating silently is the state in which a payment business discovers a problem months late and cannot say how much money it involves.
Automate the matching, escalate the exceptions. The great majority match on reference and amount. Engineering effort belongs in classifying the remainder and in making each class self-explanatory to the operations team.
Settlement and payouts
  • Funds arrive net of fees and any withheld reserve — the gross figure your system shows is never what lands
  • Payout schedule is a product decision: daily, weekly, or on demand for a fee
  • Rolling reserve — a percentage held against future chargebacks, released on a schedule. Model it explicitly or merchant balances will not reconcile
  • Payout is itself a payment — idempotent, ledgered, reconciled, and with its own approval path for large amounts
  • Fee computation must be reproducible — record the rate applied and the basis, not just the result
The dispute lifecycle
StageWhat happens
Retrieval requestThe issuer asks for information before a formal dispute
ChargebackFunds are pulled back, plus a fee. You are debited first and argue afterwards
RepresentmentYou submit evidence — delivery proof, authentication result, terms accepted, communication history
Pre-arbitration / arbitrationEscalation, with rising costs on both sides
Deadlines are hard limits, not targets. Miss the response window and the dispute is lost automatically regardless of the merits. Build the timer into the system, alert well before expiry, and treat it as an availability concern rather than a support task.
Watch the chargeback ratio. Card networks impose monitoring programmes above threshold ratios, with fines and — at the extreme — loss of the ability to accept cards. It is one of the few engineering metrics that can end a business line.

Note 402 for a decline. A declined payment is a successful API call with a business outcome — not a 500. Conflating the two makes every merchant's error handling wrong, and makes your own alerting meaningless.
Create a payment
POST /v1/payments Authorization: Bearer sk_live_… Idempotency-Key: 01J8F3K2M9Q { "amount": { "value": 249900, "currency": "INR" }, "method": { "type": "card", "token": "tok_9f21…" }, "capture": false, // authorise only "reference": "order_1183", // merchant's id "customer": "cus_44", "metadata": { "channel": "web" } } HTTP/1.1 201 Created { "id": "pay_8f3c1a", "status": "AUTHORISED", "amount": { "value": 249900, "currency": "INR" }, "captured": { "value": 0, "currency": "INR" }, "authExpiresAt": "2026-09-08T10:14:22Z", "method": { "brand": "visa", "last4": "4242" }, "riskScore": 12, "createdAt": "2026-09-01T10:14:22Z" }
Returning authExpiresAt is a small thing that prevents a common failure. It tells the merchant when the hold lapses, so late captures can be scheduled or abandoned deliberately rather than discovered as an error.
A decline, and an error
HTTP/1.1 402 Payment Required { "id": "pay_8f3c1b", "status": "DECLINED", "declineCode": "insufficient_funds", "issuerMessage": "Do not honour", "retryable": false, "requestId": "req_77a2" } HTTP/1.1 409 Conflict { "code": "IDEMPOTENCY_IN_PROGRESS", "message": "Original request still processing", "retryAfter": 2, "requestId": "req_77a3" }
The retryable flag earns its place. Merchants cannot be expected to maintain a mapping of every issuer decline code. Telling them plainly whether another attempt could succeed prevents both pointless retries and abandoned recoverable payments.
Webhooks — both directions
  • Signed with a shared secret and a timestamp; reject anything outside a small clock window
  • At-least-once and unordered — deduplicate by event id and ignore stale transitions
  • Retried with backoff over hours, then parked for manual replay
  • A replay endpoint so a merchant can recover after their own outage
  • Never the only channel — the payment status endpoint must always be authoritative

What breaks first — the bottleneck ladder
#Limit
1The PSP's rate limit. External, and you cannot scale past it. Negotiate headroom before a sale, not during one
2Issuer latency dragging p99 past your client timeout — which manufactures UNKNOWN states faster than the resolver clears them
3Ledger single-writer throughput per account. The only one you can fix with your own infrastructure
4Reconciliation runtime exceeding its window, so breaks are found a day late and compound
5Webhook delivery backlog when a large merchant's endpoint degrades
Note that only one of the five is yours. Payment capacity planning is mostly about other people's limits, which is why the conversation with the provider matters more than the autoscaling configuration.
What I would monitor above all
  • Authorisation rate, sliced by route, BIN and issuer — it moves without any deploy
  • Payments in UNKNOWN, and their age distribution
  • Unexplained reconciliation breaks, and their total value
  • Duplicate detection rate — a rising number means idempotency is being tested and holding
  • Time to dispute deadline for anything open
The one alert that matters most is a fall in authorisation rate. It is the earliest signal of a provider problem, an issuer problem, a fraud-rule regression or an attack — and it is a revenue metric, so it gets attention.

The one that summarises the sheet: kill the network mid-authorisation, then answer three questions — was the customer charged, what does your database say, and what will the settlement file say tomorrow? If those three answers can disagree without anything alerting, the system is not ready to take money.
