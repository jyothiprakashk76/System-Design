The most common interview failure is not a wrong component — it is designing for unstated scale. Sharding a database that holds 40 GB, or hand-waving a flash sale that is the actual hard part. Name the numbers, then design to them.

Always say “p99”, never “average”. An average hides a bimodal distribution completely, and it is the tail that customers abandon carts over.
Availability arithmetic worth knowing
SLADowntime / year
99%3.65 days
99.9%8.8 hours
99.99%52.6 minutes
99.999%5.3 minutes — very expensive, and rarely justified for retail
Serial dependencies multiply, and this is the number people get wrong. A checkout that must call five services each at 99.9% has a composite availability of roughly 99.5% — about 44 hours a year. Quoting 99.99% for a system with a long synchronous dependency chain is arithmetic, not ambition.
Which is why the design removes dependencies from the critical path. Every service you can make asynchronous, cache, or degrade gracefully around raises the composite number. That is the real reason for the event-driven parts of this architecture — not decoupling for its own sake.

// stated assumptions — say these out loud DAU 10,000,000 sessions/user/day 1.5 page views/session 20 → 300 M views/day average RPS 300M / 86,400 ≈ 3,500 rps peak (evening, ×5) ≈ 17,500 rps flash sale (×50 for minutes) ≈ 175,000 rps // write path is far smaller conversion ~2% → 300,000 orders/day → ~3.5 orders/sec average → ~35/sec at peak // read:write ≈ 100:1 — the single most // important ratio in the whole design
Storage
Catalogue10 M products × ~5 KB = ~50 GB. Fits in memory on one machine — this is not big data, and does not need sharding
Orders300 K/day × 2 KB × 365 = ~220 GB/year, growing linearly. Partition by time
Images10 M × 5 × 200 KB = ~10 TB — object storage plus a CDN, never a database
Events300 M/day × 1 KB = ~300 GB/day into Kafka. This dominates the infrastructure bill
What these numbers tell you. Reads are 100× writes, so cache aggressively and replicate reads. The catalogue is small enough to keep hot in memory. The order database does not need sharding at this scale for years. The events, not the orders, are the volume problem.
Do the arithmetic before choosing technology. Half the components on a typical e-commerce diagram are unjustified at 3,500 rps and 50 GB — and the same diagram is badly under-designed for the flash sale, which is where the real engineering is.
3  ·  CAP AND PACELC — PER CAPABILITY, NOT PER SYSTEM the framing that is usually stated wrongly, and what each part of the platform actually chooses
The correction first: CAP is not “pick two of three”. You do not choose P — network partitions happen whether you want them or not, so any distributed system must tolerate them. The real statement is narrower: during a partition, you must choose consistency or availability. When there is no partition, you get both, and Brewer himself has said the two-of-three formulation was always misleading.
PACELC is the version you actually use. If Partition → choose A or C; Else → choose Latency or Consistency. Partitions are rare; the latency-versus- consistency trade-off is made on every single request, which makes it the more useful lens day to day.
And it is chosen per capability, not per system. The same platform is CP for payment and inventory, and AP for catalogue and reviews — often within one request. Answering “our system is AP” is the sign of someone who has read about CAP rather than applied it.
A second correction worth making
MongoDB is commonly labelled AP. By default it is CP — a replica set has one primary, writes with majority write concern are confirmed by a majority, and the minority side of a partition has no primary and therefore cannot accept writes. It is tunable toward availability with weaker read and write concerns, which is exactly the point: the classification follows the configuration, not the product.
CapabilityChoiceWhy, and what it costs
Payment & ledgerCPA double charge or lost payment is unrecoverable trust damage. Refuse the write rather than risk divergence. Single-region primary, synchronous commit
Inventory decrementCPOverselling has a real cost — cancellations, compensation, reputation. Serialise on the item, or hold a reservation
Order placementCPAn acknowledged order must exist. Durability before response
User account & authCPCredential and permission changes must not be stale — a revoked session must be revoked
Product catalogueAPA price or description seconds out of date is acceptable; the storefront being down is not. Cache and replicate freely
Search indexAPNear-real-time is fine. Serving slightly stale results beats returning an error
Reviews & ratingsAPA review appearing a minute later harms nobody
RecommendationsAPStale recommendations degrade quality slightly; unavailable ones remove a whole surface
CartMixedAP while browsing — never lose a cart, tolerate a stale line. Becomes CP at checkout, when price and stock are re-validated authoritatively
The cart row is the interesting one, and worth volunteering. The same data deliberately changes consistency class at a business boundary — cheap and available while shopping, strict at the moment money is involved. That is what “per capability” means in practice.

5  ·  WHY EACH COMPONENT — THE ANSWER, AND THE FOLLOW-UP what an interviewer is testing is whether you know the cost, not the benefit
ComponentWhy  ·  and what it costs you
CDNWhy: images and static assets are most of the bytes and none of the logic. Serving from the edge cuts latency and origin load together.
Cost: cache invalidation becomes a distributed problem; use immutable filenames and version the URL rather than purging
WAF / DDoSWhy: a public storefront is permanently under scraping and credential-stuffing traffic.
Cost: false positives block real customers; tune in monitor mode before enforcing
API gatewayWhy: one place for authentication, rate limiting, quotas and versioning, instead of each service reimplementing them.
Cost: a single point of failure and a potential bottleneck — it must scale horizontally and stay stateless
MicroservicesWhy: catalogue and checkout have completely different scaling and availability profiles; separate ownership and deployment.
Cost: a distributed system, with every problem on this sheet. A modular monolith is the right answer more often than it is chosen
KafkaWhy: absorbs spikes, decouples producers from consumers, and lets many derived systems read the same facts with replay.
Cost: operational weight; ordering only per partition; consumer lag becomes a thing you monitor and page on
RedisWhy: sub-millisecond cart, session, counters and rate limits.
Cost: it is AP with async replication and can lose acknowledged writes on failover — never the source of truth for money or stock
ComponentWhy  ·  and what it costs you
PostgreSQLWhy: orders, payments and inventory need real transactions, constraints and isolation. Nothing else on the sheet is a substitute.
Cost: single-writer scaling; plan partitioning and connection pooling early
Document storeWhy: catalogue items have wildly varying attributes by category — a fixed schema fights the domain.
Cost: no joins; you denormalise, and you own keeping duplicates in step. PostgreSQL JSONB removes this component for many teams
OpenSearchWhy: relevance ranking, facets, typo tolerance and autocomplete are a different problem from querying rows.
Cost: a second copy of the catalogue to keep in step, and it must never be the source of truth
Object storageWhy: images and invoices are large, immutable and cheap to serve.
Cost: egress charges; lifecycle policies matter
Service meshWhy: mTLS, retries and circuit breaking uniformly, without touching application code.
Cost: substantial. Only adopt when you genuinely have many services — below roughly a dozen, libraries are simpler
KubernetesWhy: bin-packing, autoscaling, rolling deploys and self-healing.
Cost: a platform to operate. Managed compute is often sufficient and dramatically cheaper in engineering time
The answer that distinguishes a staff-level response. For every component, state what you would need to see before adding it, and what you would remove first. “We use Kafka because it decouples services” is a brochure. “We use Kafka because eight consumers need the same order events with replay, and below that a database table and a poller would do” is engineering.
6  ·  INVENTORY & OVERSELL the hardest correctness problem
State the problem precisely. Ten thousand customers click buy on the last 100 units within the same second. Read-then-write without serialisation means many of them read “100 available” and all of them succeed. Overselling is a business incident: cancellations, compensation, and in some jurisdictions a regulatory one.
Three levels of stock
On handPhysically in the warehouse
ReservedHeld for in-flight checkouts, with an expiry
Availableon_hand − reserved — the only number a customer may act on
Making the decrement safe
-- atomic and correct: the DB serialises it UPDATE inventory SET available = available - :qty WHERE sku = :sku AND available >= :qty; -- 0 rows affected = insufficient stock, cleanly -- and the reservation, with an expiry INSERT INTO reservation(order_id, sku, qty, expires_at) VALUES (…, now() + interval '15 minutes');
Why not a distributed lock? A lock with a TTL is not mutual exclusion — a stalled holder can have its lease expire while it still believes it holds it, giving two writers. A conditional update inside the database is simpler, cheaper and actually correct.
Flash sale — the hot-key problem
  • One row becomes the bottleneck. Every buyer contends on the same SKU, and lock waits collapse throughput
  • Shard the counter — split 1,000 units into 10 buckets of 100 and pick one at random. Contention drops tenfold; the cost is a small chance of a false sold-out while another bucket has stock
  • Admission control — a queue or token gate in front, so only as many requests reach inventory as there is stock to sell
  • Fail fast and honestly when sold out; do not queue a customer behind an outcome that is already decided
The expiry is what makes it self-healing. A crashed checkout releases its stock automatically. Without a TTL, every abandoned cart leaks inventory permanently and someone reconciles it by hand.
7  ·  PAYMENTS where mistakes are unrecoverable
The governing fact. A timeout from a payment provider tells you nothing about whether the customer was charged. The request may have succeeded and the response been lost. Every design decision below follows from that one sentence.
Non-negotiables
  • Idempotency key per payment attempt, generated when the intent is formed and sent to the PSP. A retry with the same key returns the original result rather than charging again
  • Order created before payment, so a successful charge always has a record to attach to
  • Money as integer minor units with an explicit currency. Never floating point
  • An append-only ledger — payment state is derived from entries, not overwritten in place
  • Card data never touches your services. Tokenise at the PSP; this is what keeps PCI scope small
The states, and the awkward one
INITIATED ─▶ AUTHORISED ─▶ CAPTURED ─▶ SETTLED │ │ └▶ REFUNDED │ └▶ VOIDED └▶ FAILED UNKNOWN ← timeout. Not a failure. Must be resolved by query or reconciliation, never by assuming and retrying blindly.
Webhooks and reconciliation
  • Verify the signature on every webhook; treat the payload as untrusted otherwise
  • Webhooks are at-least-once and can arrive out of order — deduplicate by event id and ignore transitions older than current state
  • Never trust a webhook alone. Poll the PSP for anything left in UNKNOWN
  • Daily reconciliation against the provider's settlement file, with a report of every mismatch. This is how double charges are actually caught
Authorise then capture, rather than charging immediately. Authorisation holds the funds while you confirm stock and fraud checks; capture on dispatch. It makes cancellation cheap and is what most retail flows do.
8  ·  CACHING & SEARCH absorbing the 100:1 ratio
Invalidation — the actual hard part
StrategyWhen
Short TTLDefault. Simple, self-correcting, and bounded staleness you can state
Event-drivenPrice and stock changes publish an event; consumers evict. Fast, and the invalidation itself can fail
Versioned keyInclude a version in the key so a new version is a cache miss rather than a delete race
Write-throughFor data that must never be stale — rare on the read path here
Three failure modes that cause real outages. Stampede — a popular key expires and thousands of requests hit the database at once; fix with jittered TTLs and a single-flight lock. Hot key — one product saturates one Redis node; fix by replicating the key across nodes or caching in-process. Cold start — after a restart the cache is empty and the database receives traffic it was never sized for; warm before taking traffic.
Search indexing pipeline
catalogue write ─▶ outbox ─▶ Kafka ─▶ indexer ─▶ OpenSearch │ └▶ vector index // never dual-write to DB and index directly: // that is the dual-write problem, and it silently // leaves the index wrong with no error
The index is a projection, never the source of truth. It must be fully rebuildable from the catalogue, and rebuilding should be a routine operation you have actually practised — because you will need it after a mapping change.
Hybrid retrieval is now standard: keyword search for exact terms, model numbers and brand names; vector search for intent and synonyms. Fuse the two ranked lists. Keyword alone fails on “something warm for winter”; vector alone fails on a part number.
9  ·  DEGRADATION MATRIX what still works when X is down
Decide this at design time, not during the incident. Each row is a deliberate product decision about what the customer sees.
DownBehaviour
SearchFall back to category browse and the cached bestseller list. Never a blank page
RecommendationsHide the widget entirely. It is decoration, and a spinner is worse than an absence
ReviewsShow the cached aggregate rating; hide the review list
CacheServe from the database with a circuit breaker and load shedding. Expect a hard capacity drop and shed browse first
InventoryStop accepting checkout. Selling stock you cannot verify is worse than a clear error
Payment providerOffer an alternative provider if integrated; otherwise save the cart, tell the truth, and invite the customer back
Order serviceCheckout is down. Keep browse fully available — it is a different failure domain by design
NotificationNothing customer-visible. Events queue and drain later
AnalyticsNothing customer-visible. Never let analytics block a request
The principle running through the table. Browse degrades gracefully; checkout fails loudly and safely. Never take money you cannot honour, and never show a customer an empty page when a cached approximation exists.
Test these paths deliberately. Fallbacks are the least-exercised code in the system and run precisely when everything else is already failing. A fallback that has never been executed is a hypothesis.

Conventions applied to every endpoint
VersioningIn the path. A breaking change is a new version; existing consumers keep working
PaginationCursor, not offset. Offset pagination drifts when data changes underneath and degrades badly at depth
IdempotencyRequired on every unsafe retryable POST. Stored with the result; a repeat returns the original response, not a conflict
ConcurrencyETag + If-Match on mutable resources, returning 412 on mismatch
Rate limitsX-RateLimit-* headers and 429 with Retry-After — tell the client how to behave
TracingX-Request-ID accepted and echoed; propagated to every downstream hop
One error envelope, everywhere
HTTP/1.1 409 Conflict { "code": "INSUFFICIENT_STOCK", "message": "Only 2 units available", "requestId": "req_8f3c…", "details": [{ "sku": "A1", "available": 2 }] }
A stable machine-readable code is what clients branch on — never the human message, which is free to change and to be localised.
Note the status codes on the write path. POST /orders returns 201 synchronously because the customer must know the order exists; cancel and payment return 202 because they complete asynchronously. Choosing between them honestly is more informative than any response body.
Place an order — the request that matters
POST /v1/orders Authorization: Bearer eyJ… Idempotency-Key: 01J8F3… ← client-generated, X-Request-ID: req_8f3c… one per intent { "cartId": "c_9912", "addressId": "a_44", "paymentMethodId": "pm_tok_77", "expectedTotal": { "amount": 249900, "currency": "INR" } } HTTP/1.1 201 Created { "orderId": "o_1183", "status": "PENDING", "total": { "amount": 249900, "currency": "INR" }, "payment": { "id": "pay_552", "status": "PENDING" }, "reservationExpiresAt": "2026-09-01T14:15:00Z" }
expectedTotal is the important field. The server re-prices authoritatively and compares. A mismatch returns 422 with the new total rather than silently charging a different amount — which is what turns a stale-price bug into a handled case.
Money is an object, not a number. Integer minor units plus an explicit currency. A bare 2499.00 in JSON invites floating-point handling somewhere down the chain.
The reservation expiry is returned to the client so the interface can show a countdown. Making the hold visible is what stops customers believing checkout has silently failed.

What breaks first — the bottleneck ladder
Be ready for this follow-up. Naming the sequence, and how each is detected, is more convincing than any diagram.
#LimitDetected by
1Database connection pool. Saturates long before CPU does, and scaling pods makes it worseConnection wait time; refused connections while CPU is idle
2Hot SKU row during a sale — lock contention on one row collapses throughputLock wait time; p99 on one endpoint only
3Cache capacity and cold start — eviction rate climbs, hit ratio falls, load lands on the databaseHit ratio; eviction rate; origin request rate
4Kafka consumer lag on derived systems — silent, and it degrades search and dashboards firstLag per consumer group

Note the order. The limit people plan for — write throughput — is last. The one that actually takes systems down is first, and it is a configuration number rather than a hardware one.
The trade-offs I would revisit. Whether the document store earns its place over JSONB; whether a mesh is justified at this service count; whether Kafka retention is set from cost rather than from replay need. Volunteering what you would reconsider is a stronger close than defending every choice.

The honest summary an interviewer is listening for. At 3,500 rps and a 50 GB catalogue, a well-tuned PostgreSQL, Redis, a CDN and a modular monolith would serve this workload with far less operational surface. Everything above is justified by organisational scale — many teams deploying independently — as much as by traffic. Saying so is a strength, not a concession.
